Day 1: Introduction to Neuroscience and Arduino IDE¶

Objective: Transition from biological concepts to hardware basics.

Hour 1: Getting Started¶

Welcome to the Upside Down Labs neuroscience workshop! Over the next 5 days, you will discover how your own body generates electricity — and how to record and use those signals to control machines.

What you will build towards:

  • Recording your brain waves, muscle signals, heart beats, and eye movements

  • Building real Brain-Computer Interfaces (BCIs)

  • Playing games and controlling devices with your body signals

Hardware you’ll use:

  • DIY Neuroscience Kit - Pro — for recording biosignals

  • BLE BCI Car + NPG Lite (Ninja Kit) — for BCI games and experiments

Hour 2: The Electricity Inside You¶

Your body runs on electricity. Every time you think, move, blink, or feel your heart beat, your nervous system is generating tiny electrical signals. In this session we explore where those signals come from.

The Human Nervous System¶

Think of your nervous system as the internet of your body — it sends messages between your brain and every part of your body at speeds over 120 metres per second.

It has two main parts:

  • Central Nervous System (CNS): Your brain and spinal cord. This is the command centre.

  • Peripheral Nervous System (PNS): All the nerves branching out from the spinal cord to your muscles, skin, organs, and senses.

The Brain

The brain has three major regions:

Region

What it does

Cerebrum

Thinking, memory, movement, speech, and your senses. Divided into four lobes.

Cerebellum

Balance and coordination. It has more neurons than the rest of the brain, even though it is only 10% of the brain’s volume.

Brainstem

Controls automatic functions like heart rate, breathing, and blood pressure.

Neurons — The Messengers

Neurons are the cells that carry electrical signals. They are the longest cells in the human body — a single neuron can be over 1 metre long!

Key parts of a neuron:

  • Dendrites — receive signals from other neurons

  • Cell body (Soma) — the core of the neuron

  • Axon — sends the signal forward

  • Myelin sheath — insulates the axon, making signals travel faster

  • Synapse — the tiny gap where one neuron passes a signal to the next

Fun fact: The brain contains about 86 billion neurons connected by 100 trillion synapses. Despite being only 2% of your body weight, it uses 20% of your body’s total energy.

Biopotential Signals¶

When groups of neurons or muscle cells fire together, they create electrical signals strong enough to be detected on your skin surface. These are called biopotential signals.

Signal

Full Name

Source

Typical Range

EEG

Electroencephalogram

Brain (neurons)

10–100 µV, 0.5–100 Hz

EMG

Electromyogram

Skeletal muscles

0–10 mV, 0–500 Hz

ECG

Electrocardiogram

Heart muscle

10 µV–5 mV, 0.05–100 Hz

EOG

Electrooculogram

Eyes (cornea-retina)

100–3500 µV, 0.1–20 Hz

These signals are incredibly small (microvolts = millionths of a volt), so our BioAmp hardware amplifies them by up to 1000× before we can read them.

Real-world applications:

  • EEG → Diagnose epilepsy, control prosthetic limbs, meditate with neurofeedback

  • EMG → Robotic arms, prosthetics, video game controllers

  • ECG → Heart monitors, fitness trackers, portable health devices

  • EOG → Assistive tech for paralysed patients, drowsiness detection in drivers

Hour 3: Meet Your Tools¶

The DIY Neuroscience Kit

The kit contains BioAmp boards that amplify and filter biosignals so a microcontroller can read them. All hardware is open-source — the circuit diagrams and code are freely available on GitHub.

The Arduino IDE

Arduino is a simple programming platform used to read signals from your BioAmp board and send them to a computer or display.

Key concepts:

  • setup() — runs once when the Arduino starts

  • loop() — runs forever, reading and sending data

  • Serial.begin(115200) — opens communication with your PC at 115200 bits per second

  • analogRead(A0) — reads the biosignal voltage from pin A0

Overview of the Arduino IDE¶

The Arduino IDE (Integrated Development Environment) is the software you use to write, edit, and upload code (called “sketches”) to your Arduino board. It provides:

  • A code editor for writing Arduino programs

  • A message area for errors and status

  • A text console for output

  • A toolbar with buttons for common functions (verify, upload, open, save, etc.)

  • A menu to select your board type and the correct port

Understanding the Arduino Board Layout and Pin Functions¶

An Arduino board (like the Uno) has:

  • Digital pins (0–13): Used for digital input/output (HIGH/LOW)

  • Analog pins (A0–A5): Used for reading analog signals (like biosignals)

  • Power pins: 3.3V, 5V, GND for powering sensors and modules

  • USB port: For programming and serial communication

  • Reset button: Restarts the program

Uploading Your First Sketch¶

To upload your first sketch:

  1. Connect your Arduino board to your computer via USB.

  2. Open the Arduino IDE.

  3. Select the correct board and port from the Tools menu.

  4. Write or open a sketch (e.g., the Blink example).

  5. Click the Upload button (right arrow icon).

  6. Wait for the “Done uploading” message.

If the onboard LED blinks, your upload was successful!

Activity: Navigate the GitHub repository and upload your first sketch to Arduino.

Section 2: Arduino Basics & Electronics¶

Which Arduino board is most popular for beginners?
AArduino Uno
BArduino Nano Every 33 IoT
CRaspberry Pi
DESP8266
Explanation: The Arduino Uno is the most widely used beginner board due to its simplicity, large community, and abundant learning resources.
If an LED connected to Arduino suddenly glows, what is MOST likely true?
AThe pin became HIGH
BWiFi is connected
CThe battery exploded
DThe monitor is ON
Explanation: An LED glows when current flows through it. This happens when the connected pin is set HIGH, providing voltage and allowing current to flow through the LED.
Which pin on Arduino is commonly used to provide power to sensors?
AGND
BRX
C5V
DTX
Explanation: The 5V pin provides a stable 5-volt power supply from the Arduino's onboard regulator, commonly used to power sensors and modules.
A student connects both wires of an LED to GND. What will happen?
ALED becomes brighter
BLED turns ON
CLED stays OFF
DArduino starts beeping
Explanation: For current to flow through an LED, one wire must connect to a positive voltage (like 5V or a HIGH pin) and the other to GND. With both wires on GND, there is no potential difference, so no current flows and the LED stays off.
Which function runs repeatedly in an Arduino program?
Asetup()
Bloop()
Cstart()
Dbegin()
Explanation: The loop() function runs continuously after setup() completes. All ongoing tasks — reading sensors, sending data, checking conditions — go inside loop().
Which function runs only once after Arduino powers ON?
Arepeat()
Bloop()
Csetup()
Dexecute()
Explanation: The setup() function executes once when the Arduino starts or resets. It is used for one-time initialization — setting pin modes, starting serial communication, etc.
Which command would you MOST likely use to read a sensor value?
AdigitalWrite()
BanalogRead()
CSerial.begin()
Dtone()
Explanation: analogRead() reads the voltage on an analog pin (A0–A5) and returns a value between 0 and 1023, corresponding to 0–5V. This is how biosignals from BioAmp boards are read.
